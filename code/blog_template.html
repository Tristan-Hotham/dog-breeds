Ask a kennel club in Paris for France's favourite dog and you get a dog from the Scottish Highlands. Ask in New York and you get a dog from Paris. I mapped where {{meta.n_study|int}} dog breeds were born and set them against where people register them today.

Dog breeds are some of the most travelled things people have ever made. Almost every breed you know was fixed in a stud book in one country, mostly in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and then exported everywhere. Each one comes with a birthplace: a Scottish estate, a Northumberland mining town, the Paris lace trade, a Mexican state. So it is possible to ask a simple question with open data. Where were the world's breeds born, and where are they loved now?

I took every breed in Wikidata and joined it to the international breed register kept by the Fédération Cynologique Internationale (FCI), which gives each breed it recognises one official country of origin. After removing crossbreeds, extinct breeds and duplicates, that leaves {{meta.n_study|int}} breeds. Then I gathered the latest open registration figures from {{meta.n_clubs|int}} national kennel clubs, from the Royal Kennel Club's ten-year tables to a Swedish press release.

Where breeds are born

Britain is the birthplace of more breeds than anywhere else: {{origins.top.0.n|int}}. France follows with {{origins.top.1.n|int}}, then the United States ({{origins.top.2.n|int}}), Spain ({{origins.top.3.n|int}}) and Germany ({{origins.top.4.n|int}}). Europe accounts for {{origins.europe_share|pct}} of all the breeds on the map. Spain's high count is partly about record keeping: it has many regional breeds recognised at home but not internationally, and they are well covered in Wikidata. Large parts of Africa and Asia have dog types that never went through a stud book, so they barely show.

Britain is detailed enough to zoom into. For {{uk.n_points|int}} native breeds, the English Wikipedia article names a county, valley, town or estate: the Airedale's valley of the River Aire, the mining town of Bedlington, Patterdale in the Lake District, Clumber Park in Nottinghamshire, the Guisachan estate where the Golden Retriever was bred.

The pattern is striking: terriers of the Lake District, Northumberland, Yorkshire and the Borders; gundogs and herding dogs of Scottish estates and islands. The south of England, for all its wealth, produced fewer named breeds.

Where breeds are loved

Of the {{meta.n_clubs|int}} kennel clubs, {{clubs.n_top_abroad|int}} have a number one breed that was born in another country, and {{clubs.n_top_gbr|int}} of those are British. The Golden Retriever tops the French and Australian registers; the Labrador tops Sweden, Canada and Finland as well as Britain. Only {{clubs.home_top_list|raw}} put a home-grown dog first.

Across all the clubs' top tens, {{clubs.top10_abroad_share|pct}} of places go to breeds from another country. Japan's top ten has no Japanese breed at all. It is led by the Poodle ({{clubs.top.JPN.count|int}} registrations in 2025), which the FCI files under France, and the Chihuahua from Mexico.

The French Bulldog's round trip

The French Bulldog is the clearest case of a breed loved far from home. It tops the American Kennel Club's list for the fourth year running and is seventh in Japan, but it is not in the French top three. In Britain it even overtook the Labrador for a year: {{frenchie.uk_2018|int}} registrations in 2018 against {{frenchie.lab_2018|int}} Labradors.

Then the boom ended. British French Bulldog registrations peaked at {{frenchie.uk_peak|int}} in {{frenchie.uk_peak_year|raw}} and fell to {{frenchie.uk_2025|int}} in 2025, down {{frenchie.uk_drop|pct}}. Germany is a useful reminder of what club figures miss: the VDH's member clubs bred just {{frenchie.vdh_2025|int}} French Bulldog puppies in 2025, which tells you where Germany's French Bulldogs are not bred, not how many there are.

How home-grown is your dog?

Where a club counts every breed, I can add up how much of the register is home-grown. Britain keeps {{clubs.home.GBR|pct}} of its registrations at home and Germany's VDH breeders {{clubs.home.DEU|pct}}. Australia, despite the Kelpie and the Cattle Dog, is at {{clubs.home.AUS|pct}}, with British breeds making up {{clubs.home_parts.AUS.GBR|pct}} of its register. Japan is at {{clubs.home.JPN|pct}}.

What about everywhere else?

Most countries publish no open breed figures, so on the map I fill the gaps with a stand-in: the breed whose Wikipedia article was read most in the country's main language over the last year. It is shown hatched, because it measures curiosity rather than ownership. Where I could test it against a club, in {{proxy.n_check|int}} countries, it never once named the club's own number one. People read about the breeds that are in the news or that feel like their own: the Cane Corso is the most read breed in {{proxy.cane_n|int}} of {{meta.n_langs|int}} language editions, including English, and Japanese readers look up the Shiba Inu, Finnish readers the Finnish Lapphund and Turkish readers the Kangal. Treat the hatched countries as a rough guide to what people look up.

How I did this

Breeds come from Wikidata ({{meta.n_wd|int}} items retrieved on 28 October 2026) joined to the FCI nomenclature ({{meta.n_fci|int}} breeds). Origin is the FCI country of origin where the FCI recognises a breed, otherwise Wikidata's or English Wikipedia's. Historic places are mapped to today's countries, so England, Scotland and Wales become the UK. Registration figures come from each club's own tables or press releases; where a club lists varieties separately, such as the six Dachshunds of the Royal Kennel Club, I added them together. Clubs count different things, and registered pedigree dogs are a minority of all dogs. The two registers also disagree about origins now and then: the Siberian Husky is American to the FCI and Russian to Wikidata.
